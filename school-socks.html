The Secret in My School Socks

A memoir sample by Lucky David

I was in primary 4 when my period started. I can’t remember exactly how old I was, but I decided to keep it to myself. I was very insecure, maybe because I had once told my elder sister that hers smelled. She reacted the way big sisters do and said mine would soon come, never knowing it already had.

Looking back, it’s a funny memory. And if I remember clearly, it wasn’t painful at all. Only secret.

In those days, rags were the order of the day. We were a middle-class family, but pads were a cost most households avoided when a free alternative existed. My aunty was a tailor, so there was never a shortage of rags. I hated using rags, so I often wore my pants with nothing to hold back the flow. I was just starting out, so my flow was mild.

I wore my socks to school for inspection, which was done at the gate. After that, I took them off and used them to control my flow, then rushed down to the bathroom during break to throw them away. My school uniform was navy blue, and even when I got stained, it looked like ordinary water stains that dried quickly. All of this was my secret. Nobody knew. The fear of admitting that my childhood was slipping away, and the embarrassment of constantly checking whether I was stained, kept me silent.

Then one evening, my sisters and I came back from school hungry. Like most Nigerian kids, we loved eating Baba’s leftover food, so we changed quickly and gathered around it. It felt good to be home, out of the dreaded school uniform after long hours of wearing it. I couldn’t wait to put on fresh home clothes, so I hopped into my favourite white skirt. I was on my period that day, but I was hungry, and hunger won.

I dragged over the wooden mortar we used as a stool and sat down carelessly, too hungry to think, and ate with my sisters. When we finished, my father noticed the blood on my skirt and told me to go and show it to my mother. I knew exactly what it was, but I pretended I didn’t.

There were many people in the compound that evening. My face felt hot and my legs felt heavy, but I kept my eyes on the ground, looking naive, and walked past every one of them. My own small walk of shame.

On the outside, it was a naive girl walking to ask her mother what the blood stain on her skirt was.

On the inside, it was a young girl clothed in shame, her heart racing and her mind a windy mess, but she kept taking one step after another.

When I got to where my mother was seated, picking leaves, I showed her the stain. “Mama, see.” With her head bowed, she told me to go and take my bath. Then she prepared rags for me and showed me how to use them.

A few days later, she noticed I kept throwing them away. She told me not to throw them away, but to wash and reuse them instead, and promised that if I really couldn’t manage, she would buy pads once she had money.

My elder sister mocked me: “Welcome to the club!” She had no idea I had been in the club for a long time.

Nobody ever sat me down and explained what was happening to my body. So I did what children do with things they have no words for: I hid it, in socks, in silence, in a skirt I loved too much to give up. Looking back, the blood was never the shame. The silence was.
